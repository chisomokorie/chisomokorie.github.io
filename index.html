Find the real bottleneck

Not where the process assumes the problem is, where it actually stalls.

Prove it small

One low-risk pilot, scoped narrow, before asking anyone to trust it at scale.

Make ownership visible

Selected Work

Six stories. One recurring pattern.

Each diagram visualizes the actual mechanism of the fix, not a generic icon.

The Problem

Four graduate program streams, each with its own Chair, timetable needs, and policy edge cases, all routing through one coordinator with no formal authority over any of them.

The Complexity

Twenty-plus stakeholders, two standing committees, and no shared system for tracking who needed what, by when, or why a request mattered.

The Move

Built a coordination rhythm around how each Chair actually works, rather than forcing one process onto four different working styles, and became the translation point between a Chair's reasoning and the case the Associate Dean's office needs to see.

Concrete Example

When an international student's Chair-approved request for part-time status reached the Associate Dean, an immigration concern was raised. Rather than treat that as final, a RISIA-certified advisor at Western confirmed in writing that no concern applied, turning a verbal reassurance into evidence that secured approval.

Coordination without authority works when you become the most reliable source of accurate context, not the person enforcing compliance.

The Problem

Course-approval requests at King's routinely stalled, not where the process assumed delay would happen, but at a specific step nobody had flagged.

The Move

Identified the exact step causing the loop before it was formally flagged as an issue, corrected it, and coordinated adoption of the fix across all five academic departments rather than fixing it locally.

Mechanism

The gap was found by observing where requests actually stalled, not where the documented process said they should move quickly, a distinction that made the fix targeted rather than a broad, disruptive overhaul.

15%faster turnaround
5departments
The fastest fixes come from watching where work actually stalls, not from redesigning the whole process on assumption.

The Problem

A short-staffed team was drowning in manual meeting-notes work, and a broad AI rollout would have met immediate resistance.

The Move

Proposed a generative AI pilot scoped to one low-risk use case rather than asking for a full rollout. A Chair had unsuccessfully sought similar approval for about a year before this narrower framing succeeded.

Outcome

The pilot's success earned enough trust to expand the tool into the team's broader workflow, and that same trust carried over when AI was later used to fix confusing language on the department website.

Broad trust in a new technology is earned in narrow, provable steps, never asked for upfront.

About
LONDON, ON

I keep finding myself in situations where people, processes, and priorities do not naturally line up: a policy exception with no clear owner, a workflow everyone complains about but no one has fixed, a new tool nobody trusts yet.

That instinct started earlier than my career did. I began university in Economics, completing coursework in linear algebra, calculus, and micro and macroeconomics, before transitioning into Sociology, Art History, and Studio Art. Understanding a pattern in data is one thing; understanding why people actually behave the way that data shows is another, and that second lens has shaped how I approach every problem since.

That instinct has developed across academic administration, municipal operations, and independent creative work. Different environments, same underlying problem: understand what people actually need, make the moving parts visible, align the right people, and create a path forward.

That is the work I like most: taking something complicated and making it move.

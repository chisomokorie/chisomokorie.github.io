How I work.

Five moves, repeated across every story below, regardless of the industry.

Find the real bottleneck

Not where the process assumes the problem is, where it actually stalls.

Prove it small

One low-risk pilot, scoped narrow, before asking anyone to trust it at scale.

Make ownership visible

Someone specific accountable, not a diffuse group hoping it works out.

Protect the outcome

Not the task list. The task list is negotiable; the outcome is not.

Let evidence earn the next step

Trust is built by proof, not by asking for it upfront.

Selected Work

Eleven stories. One recurring pattern.

Each diagram visualizes the actual mechanism of the fix, not a generic icon.

The Problem

Four graduate program streams, each with its own Chair, timetable needs, and policy edge cases, all routing through one coordinator with no formal authority over any of them.

The Complexity

Twenty-plus stakeholders, three standing committees, and no shared system for tracking who needed what, by when, or why a request mattered.

The Move

Built a coordination rhythm around how each Chair actually works, rather than forcing one process onto four different working styles, and became the translation point between a Chair's reasoning and the case the Associate Dean's office needs to see.

Concrete Example

When an international student's Chair-approved request for part-time status reached the Associate Dean, an immigration concern was raised. Rather than treat that as final, a RISIA-certified advisor at Western confirmed in writing that no concern applied, turning a verbal reassurance into evidence that secured approval.

20+stakeholders coordinated
4graduate Chairs
30+students, orientation session
Coordination without authority works when you become the most reliable source of accurate context, not the person enforcing compliance.

The Problem

Course-approval requests at King's routinely stalled, not where the process assumed delay would happen, but at a specific step nobody had flagged.

The Move

Identified the exact step causing the loop before it was formally flagged as an issue, corrected it, and coordinated adoption of the fix across all five academic departments rather than fixing it locally.

Mechanism

The gap was found by observing where requests actually stalled, not where the documented process said they should move quickly, a distinction that made the fix targeted rather than a broad, disruptive overhaul.

15%faster turnaround
5departments
The fastest fixes come from watching where work actually stalls, not from redesigning the whole process on assumption.

The Problem

Meeting notes took the team significant manual time, and a broad AI rollout would have met immediate resistance.

The Move

Proposed a generative AI pilot scoped to one low-risk use case rather than asking for a full rollout. There was initial hesitation, so starting small made it easier to say yes.

Outcome

The pilot's success earned enough trust to expand the tool into the team's broader workflow, and that same trust carried over when AI was later used to fix confusing language on the department website.

Broad trust in a new technology is earned in narrow, provable steps, never asked for upfront.

The Problem

The Move

5,000+requests managed

The Problem

A new rule meant no department courses could clash at all, and some faculty pushed back, wanting the times most convenient for them.

The Move

I held the rule and built an Excel timetable dashboard that shows a course conflict before it happens, so faculty could see the clash for themselves instead of taking my word for it. I owned the process from start to finish.

93%conflict-free course schedule
People trust what they can verify themselves far more than what they are told, however convincingly.

The Problem

Building a personal content brand across Instagram, TikTok, YouTube, Facebook and Snapchat with no ad budget and no guarantee that any video would travel.

The Move

Read each platform’s insights to see who was watching, from where, and at what time. TikTok showed a window when followers were most active, so I posted only then, and kept a consistent style and schedule so both the algorithm and the audience knew what to expect.

Result

Reach grew sharply, entirely organically, to 11,000+ followers and 1M+ views across platforms over more than five years, with most of the reach on TikTok.

The Problem

Graduate students move through admission, advising, awards, thesis and graduation, and a missed requirement at any step can cost them time, funding or a job.

The Move

The Problem

The Chair I supported gave speakers King’s and department merchandise to build long-standing relationships. I managed the department’s merchandise inventory and procurement, and we needed more to cover every guest that school year. King’s had ended free merchandise, the department was trying to save money, and the King’s store prices were high.

The Move

I asked my team who to speak to, tried a contact who handled merchandise outside the store, and then used contacts from my time in Enrolment Services, whose office handles hundreds of items for open houses and tours. The first ask did not work, but I was given contacts across King’s, made a compelling case for how it would benefit King’s and the department, and followed up several times.

Result

The merchandise the department received was completely free and covered the school year.

The first no is usually information. It tells you who to ask next.

About
LONDON, ON

I keep finding myself in situations where people, processes, and priorities do not naturally line up: a policy exception with no clear owner, a workflow everyone complains about but no one has fixed, a new tool nobody trusts yet.

That instinct started earlier than my career did. I began university in Economics, completing coursework in linear algebra, calculus, and micro and macroeconomics, before transitioning into Sociology, Art History, and Studio Art. Understanding a pattern in data is one thing; understanding why people actually behave the way that data shows is another, and that second lens has shaped how I approach every problem since.

Alongside my work, I have created short-form video for more than five years, growing my own pages organically to 11,000+ followers and 1M+ views across platforms. What I learned there about timing, consistency and what an audience really responds to, I now use to help small businesses reach the people they are meant to serve, one business at a time.

That instinct has developed across academic administration, municipal operations, and independent creative work. Different environments, same underlying problem: understand what people actually need, make the moving parts visible, align the right people, and create a path forward.

That is the work I like most: taking something complicated and making it move.

LinkedIn · Program Management

Always anticipate.

As a program manager, a big part of my job is seeing problems before they arrive. That means asking the right questions early, so small issues never get the chance to become delays.

Read more

Building my first software product has taught me the same lesson from a different angle.

My app includes an AI agent with one deliberately narrow job: helping users complete a specific section. Early on, I asked myself, “What happens when someone tries to make it do more?”

Because someone will. So I set guardrails to keep the agent focused on what it was designed for, no matter how it’s asked.

The challenge is that you can only anticipate so much at once. Some problems only show up when real people use what you’ve built.

But anticipating is still worth it. It isn’t about predicting everything. It’s about being prepared enough that you can adapt quickly when something does come up. Plan for what you can see. Stay flexible for what you can’t.

Sociology taught me to study people. Art taught me to see. Both turned out to be product skills.

As I build my app with AI tools like Lovable and Claude Code, I keep coming back to what my degree trained me to do.

Sociology taught me to ask how people actually behave, not how we assume they will. So when I design a screen, I don’t stop at “Does this work?” I ask, “What will someone really do here?”

Art taught me to notice what feels clear and what feels cluttered. That shows up every time I decide what stays on a page and what goes.

Great products are built for people. And understanding people is a skill you can build in many places.

Every experience you’ve had is preparing you for something. Sometimes you only see what when you start something new.

LinkedIn · Prioritization

Not everything that arrives urgently is urgent.

One of my earliest lessons in managing complex work came from a high-volume customer service role. Requests came in all day. Each one felt like a priority to the person sending it, and every one deserved a response. But they couldn’t all go first.

Read more

So I learned to triage. Before acting on a request, I’d ask: What do the guidelines actually say? Who is affected, and how quickly? Which team is best placed to solve this?

Those three questions did more than sort a queue. They turned a constant flood of “urgent” into a clear order of work, and they meant each request reached the right people the first time.

I still use that habit today, whether I’m coordinating stakeholders or deciding what to build next in my app. When everything feels urgent, the real work is deciding what matters most.

How do you decide what comes first when everything feels like a priority?
